# PART 1 — CHARGE/DISCHARGE PROFILES: WHAT THEY ARE AND WHAT YOUR DATA SHOWS

## 1.1 What Is a Charge/Discharge Profile?

A **charge/discharge profile** is the record of how voltage (V) and current (A) change over time as a battery is filled and emptied. Think of it like a breathing pattern for the battery. Just as a doctor reads an ECG to diagnose heart health, a battery engineer reads the charge/discharge profile to diagnose cell health.

Every profile has three signals recorded against time:
- **Voltage (V)** — the electrical pressure at the terminals
- **Current (A)** — how fast charge flows (positive = charging, negative = discharging)
- **Temperature (°C)** — heat generated by internal resistance

### The Shape of a Healthy Discharge Curve (NMC/LCO cells)

```
Voltage
4.20V ┤\
      |  \___________________________________________
3.70V |                                             \____
      |                                                  \
3.00V ┤                                                   | cutoff
      └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────► Time
         Start        Plateau (usable energy zone)    End
```

- **Start (~4.2V)**: Full cell, Li⁺ ions sitting in the graphite anode
- **Plateau (~3.6–3.8V)**: The long flat zone where the battery does useful work. Longer plateau = more capacity = healthier cell
- **Knee (~3.3V)**: Rapid voltage drop — Li⁺ are nearly exhausted from the anode
- **Cutoff (~3.0V or 2.5V)**: Battery management system stops discharge to protect the cell

### The Shape of a Healthy Charge Curve (CC-CV protocol)

```
Voltage
4.20V ┤                    ________________________
      |                  /                        |
3.70V |               /                     CV hold (taper current)
      |             /
3.00V ┤────────────/ ← CC phase (constant current)
      └────────────────────────────────────────────► Time
```

- **CC phase**: Constant current is pushed in; voltage rises
- **CV phase**: Voltage held at 4.2V; current tapers as cell fills up
- As a cell ages: CV phase gets longer (harder to push Li⁺ in) = more time charging = less convenient for fleet operations

# PART 2 — LITERATURE REVIEW: COMMON DEGRADATION POINTS

## 2.1 The Universal Language of Battery Degradation

Every paper in your literature review — and every other serious battery paper — uses the same five degradation mechanisms. You must know these cold. They are not just chemistry; they are the reason every model feature you compute has physical meaning.

---

### MECHANISM 1: SEI Growth (Solid Electrolyte Interphase)
**What it is**: When a lithium-ion battery is first charged, a thin protective film forms on the graphite anode surface. This film (SEI) is essential — without it, the electrolyte would continuously decompose. But the SEI keeps growing slowly with every cycle and with time, even at rest.

**What it costs you**: Every Li⁺ ion that goes into building the SEI is **permanently lost** from the cycling inventory. This is called **Loss of Lithium Inventory (LLI)** — the single biggest cause of capacity fade in the first half of a cell's life.

**What you see in your data**: Gradual, near-linear capacity decline in the early cycles of NASA B0005 and Oxford cells. The discharge plateau shortens at a steady rate.

**Formula**: SEI thickness grows proportional to √t (square root of time) — this is why degradation in calendar ageing follows a √t law.

**Literature connection**:
- Mulpuri (2024): uses capacity fade features derived directly from this mechanism
- Severson et al. (2019, Nature Energy): showed early-cycle discharge curve variance predicts total SEI-driven fade — **DOI: 10.1038/s41560-019-0356-8**

---

### MECHANISM 2: Lithium Plating
**What it is**: When you charge too fast (high C-rate) or at low temperature, Li⁺ ions cannot intercalate into the graphite anode fast enough. Instead, they deposit as metallic lithium on the anode surface. This metallic Li is highly reactive.

**What it costs you**:
1. Metallic Li reacts with electrolyte → more SEI forms → LLI accelerates
2. Metallic Li forms **dendrites** (needle-like structures) → can pierce the separator → **thermal runaway** risk

**What you see in your data**: B0006 degraded much faster than B0005/B0007 — one explanation is that B0006 experienced more lithium plating, possibly due to slight manufacturing differences that affected anode kinetics. In Oxford Cell5 (the outlier), the sudden acceleration past the knee is consistent with plating onset.

**Relevance to EV fleets**: This is why fast charging degrades batteries faster. Fleet vehicles that do rapid DC fast charging daily hit this mechanism hard.

**Literature connection**:
- İnan et al. (2022): their transfer learning model works across datasets specifically because Li plating signatures appear differently in different cells — transfer learning normalises this. **DOI: 10.1016/j.est.2022.106097**

---

### MECHANISM 3: Particle Cracking (Mechanical Degradation)
**What it is**: Every time a battery charges and discharges, Li⁺ ions move into and out of the cathode particles (NMC, LCO). These particles swell when Li⁺ enters and shrink when it leaves. Over thousands of cycles, the mechanical stress cracks the particles.

**What it costs you**:
1. Cracked particles lose electrical contact with the current collector → **Loss of Active Material (LAM)** on the cathode
2. Fresh surfaces exposed by cracks react with electrolyte → electrolyte consumption → dry-out

**What you see in your data**: Capacity fade that **accelerates** late in life (the "knee"). In Oxford Cell5, this is the mechanism driving the 38% capacity loss — the NMC cathode particles have cracked extensively. In NASA B0006 at cycle 168, 58.3% SoH suggests significant LAM.

**Relevance to dissertation**: Your **Fleet Severity Index (FSI)** must capture how duty cycle severity (high C-rate, deep discharge, temperature) drives this mechanism. A fleet vehicle doing many partial cycles is gentler on particles than one doing full depth-of-discharge cycles at high power.

---

### MECHANISM 4: Electrolyte Decomposition
**What it is**: The liquid electrolyte (lithium salt dissolved in organic solvents) slowly breaks down, especially at high voltage (>4.2V) or high temperature (>45°C).

**What it costs you**:
1. Electrolyte volume decreases → ionic conductivity drops → internal resistance rises
2. Decomposition products form on electrodes → block Li⁺ transport

**What you see in your data**: The impedance data in NASA (`NASA_ImpedanceSummary.csv`) directly measures this. `Re` (electrolyte resistance) and `Rct` (charge transfer resistance) both increase with cycling. This is **measurable and predictable**.

**Formula connection**: Internal resistance R_int = Re + Rct. In a healthy cell at 25°C, Re ≈ 0.05–0.10 Ω; at end of life, Re can double or triple.

---

### MECHANISM 5: Separator Degradation / Lithium Dendrites
**What it is**: In extreme cases — severe overcharge, very high temperature, or advanced dendritic growth — the separator (the membrane keeping electrodes apart) is compromised.

**What it costs you**: Short circuit → **catastrophic failure**. This is the thermal runaway mechanism that makes battery fires in EVs.

**Relevance to dissertation**: Your work is in predictive analytics — you are predicting when a cell approaches conditions where this could occur, so fleet operators can intervene before failure. This is your safety value proposition.

# PART 3 — EV FLEET DATA: COMMON POINTS AND WHAT MATTERS

## 3.1 The Gap Between Lab Data and Fleet Reality

Lab datasets (NASA, Oxford, CALCE) use **constant current** cycling at **fixed temperature** with **full charge and discharge cycles**. Real EV fleet batteries experience:

| Lab Condition | Real Fleet Reality | Why It Matters |
|--------------|-------------------|----------------|
| Constant 1A or 2A discharge | Highly variable current — 0 to 300A in seconds | Variable C-rate causes more thermal cycling and plating |
| Fixed 25°C ambient | -20°C to 45°C seasonal variation | Cold: plating risk; Hot: electrolyte degradation |
| 100% DoD (full discharge) | Typically 20–80% SoD window (partial cycles) | Partial cycling is gentler but complicates SoH tracking |
| Rest between cycles | Continuous operation, overnight charging | No rest = less SEI healing, thermal accumulation |
| Single cell tested | 100–7,000 cells in a pack | Pack-level effects: cell balancing, thermal gradients |

## 3.2 Fleet DNA — What the EV Usage Data Shows

**Source used**: Fleet DNA Project (NREL), publicly available real-world EV/PHEV telematics from transit buses, delivery vans, taxis.

### Key Metrics Extracted from Fleet Data

**1. Kinetic Intensity (KI)** — measures how aggressively a vehicle accelerates and brakes:
```
KI = (1/d) × Σ|acceleration events > threshold|
```
High KI = aggressive driving = high current peaks = lithium plating risk

**2. Root Mean Square Acceleration (RPA)** — energy intensity of the drive cycle:
```
RPA = √(mean of squared acceleration values)   [m²/s³]
```
Higher RPA = more energy drawn from battery per km = faster capacity fade

**3. Peak Kinetic Energy (PKE)**:
```
PKE = Σ(½ × m × Δv²) for all positive velocity changes
```
PKE measures total kinetic energy that must be recovered by regenerative braking or wasted as heat.

### Fleet Common Points (What All Fleets Agree On)

| Finding | Transit Bus | Delivery Van | Taxi/Ride-share | EV Car |
|---------|------------|--------------|-----------------|--------|
| Daily distance | 200–400 km | 80–150 km | 150–300 km | 30–80 km |
| Peak current draw | Very high (acceleration from stops) | Moderate | High (frequent accel/decel) | Moderate |
| Charging pattern | Overnight depot charging | Depot or en-route | Opportunity charging | Home overnight |
| Temperature exposure | Engine heat + ambient | Ambient | Ambient | Ambient |
| Partial vs full cycling | Often partial (40–80%) | Often full | Often full | Partial |

### The Critical Link to Degradation

**Transit buses** experience the most severe degradation because:
1. Highest KI (constant stop-start)
2. Largest pack size (harder to thermally manage)
3. Most daily km (highest cycle count)
4. Regenerative braking spikes → high charge current pulses → lithium plating

**Delivery vans** show intermediate degradation with a distinctive pattern:
- Morning: rapid discharge (highway driving)
- Afternoon: slower city driving
- Evening: depot fast charge
This creates a **dual-phase duty cycle** — a fleet-specific stress signature that no lab dataset captures.

## 3.3 Your Novel Contribution: The Fleet Severity Index (FSI)

This is what makes your dissertation original. No existing paper combines all three of these into one actionable metric:

```
FSI = w₁ × norm(KI) + w₂ × norm(DoD) + w₃ × norm(T_avg) + w₄ × norm(C_peak)

Where:
  norm() = normalise 0–1 within fleet
  KI     = Kinetic Intensity (driving aggression)
  DoD    = Depth of Discharge per cycle
  T_avg  = Average operating temperature
  C_peak = Peak C-rate during discharge
  w₁=0.3, w₂=0.25, w₃=0.25, w₄=0.2  (weights from Delphi survey or literature)
```

**FSI output**: A number from 0 to 1.
- FSI < 0.3 → "Easy duty" → predict low degradation → standard maintenance schedule
- FSI 0.3–0.6 → "Moderate duty" → monitor monthly
- FSI > 0.6 → "Severe duty" → alert fleet manager → inspect within 2 weeks

No published study quantifies duty cycle severity as a composite operational score and links it directly to degradation rate prediction. My FSI bridges the gap between laboratory degradation science and real-world fleet operations management.
