Beyond Drive Cycles: Physics-Based Modelling of Duty-Cycle-Dependent Battery Degradation in Real-World Indian EV Scenarios
Mulpuri, B.K., Mondal, A., Kummitha, N.K. et al. · RSC Advances, 2025 · DOI: 10.1039/d5ra04379d
NMC-811 PyBaMM / SPMe Physics-based model LGM50 21700 cylindrical, 5Ah Drive cycles Degradation prediction 2025
What This Paper Does (The Core Question)

This paper asks: does the specific drive cycle a vehicle experiences matter more than the standard test cycle (WLTC) for predicting how quickly a battery degrades? The answer is: yes, significantly. Real Indian city driving (Hyderabad, Delhi, Panskura) degrades batteries 12–18% faster than the WLTC lab standard.

Degradation Mechanisms Modelled
  • SEI growth — solvent-diffusion model: side reactions on graphite anode consume lithium irreversibly → LLI
  • Lithium plating — occurs when intercalation can't keep up with current; produces reversible + irreversible (dead) lithium → LLI + capacity loss
  • Particle cracking — mechanical stress from volume changes during cycling → LAM (Loss of Active Material)

Each is modelled with differential equations inside PyBaMM. The SEI thickness grows as √(time × current), lithium plating uses Butler-Volmer kinetics, and particle cracking uses a fatigue model.

Key Results
Pattern-3 (Hyderabad aggressive) degrades the battery 12–18% faster than Pattern-S (WLTC) over the same distance. At 500 days, Pattern-3 reaches ~80% SoH (end-of-life threshold) while WLTC is still at ~87–88%. This means using WLTC for fleet battery life prediction is UNSAFE — it overestimates remaining life.
  • DS-2 (partial work charge) reduces degradation vs DS-1 (full overnight) because it avoids high-SoC dwell time
  • Temperature matters: Zone-B (Delhi extreme winter–summer swing, 1.8–47.2°C) causes more degradation than Zone-A
  • Higher charge rate (1C) causes more SEI growth and plating than 0.1C
  • LLI is the dominant mechanism (~70% of total capacity loss); particle cracking contributes ~15%; plating ~15%

Descriptive Proximity-Based Modeling with LightGBM and DBSCAN for State of Charge Estimation
İnan, A. et al. · Journal of Energy Storage, 2025 · Article 118150 · DOI: 10.1016/j.est.2025.118150
LCO/NMC 18650 LightGBM DBSCAN clustering SHAP SoC estimation Explainability 2025
What This Paper Does

This paper asks: can we improve SoC (State of Charge) estimation accuracy by first clustering the battery operating data, then training a gradient boosting model within each cluster? The key innovation is combining unsupervised clustering (DBSCAN) with supervised regression (LightGBM) — a hybrid approach the authors call "descriptive proximity-based modeling."

Important Distinction
This paper estimates SoC (how much charge remains RIGHT NOW), not SoH (long-term health). SoC changes every second; SoH changes over hundreds of cycles. However, the METHODOLOGICAL approach — DBSCAN + ML + SHAP — is directly transferable to your SoH/degradation work.
Dataset & Setup
Cell18650 cylindrical Li-ion, 2.8 Ah, tested at 0°C and 25°C Discharge rates1C, 2C, 3C, 4C — creating 8 test conditions (2 temps × 4 rates) FeaturesCurrent (A), Voltage (V), Capacity (mAh), Energy (Wh), Cycle count, Temperature (°C) DBSCAN ε parameterε = 1.21 (determined adaptively using MAD — Median Absolute Deviation of nearest-neighbour distances) ML modelLightGBM (gradient boosted decision trees, fast, handles non-linearity) Validation5-fold cross-validation; RMSE=0.188, MAE=0.145, R²=1.000 (on SoC 0–100 scale)
How DBSCAN Is Used Here

DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) groups battery measurements by proximity in feature space — it finds "natural" operating regions without needing to specify number of clusters. The adaptive ε = 1.21 was chosen as 1.5× the median nearest-neighbour distance (MAD approach), making it data-driven rather than manual. Each cluster represents a distinct operating regime (e.g., high-temperature 1C charging vs. low-temperature 4C discharge). The cluster label is then added as a feature to LightGBM.

Adding the cluster label as a feature to LightGBM allowed the model to understand that "voltage=3.6V at 25°C 1C" means something different from "voltage=3.6V at 0°C 4C." Without clustering, the model sees identical voltage readings in very different conditions and gives a single, averaged response. With clustering, it knows which regime it's in and adjusts accordingly. This is the paper's core methodological insight.

Capacity Estimation and Knee Point Prediction of Lithium Metal Batteries Using EIS and XGBoost with SHAP Interpretability
Si, Y. et al. · Advanced Science, 2025
NMC-811 (Lithium Metal) EIS XGBoost SHAP Capacity estimation Knee point detection 2025
What This Paper Does

This paper solves two related problems using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) as the input feature source: (1) estimate remaining capacity during ageing, and (2) predict when the dangerous "knee point" (KP) in the degradation curve occurs. The knee point is critical: it marks where degradation accelerates nonlinearly, and identifying it early allows fleet operators to retire or reassign batteries before sudden failure.

What Is EIS?

EIS applies a small AC voltage signal across a range of frequencies (typically 0.01 Hz to 100 kHz) and measures the battery's complex impedance response Z(ω) = Z_re + jZ_im. Different frequency ranges reveal different physical processes:

Frequency RangePhysical ProcessSymbol
Very high (>1 kHz)Ohmic resistance (electrolyte + current collector)Re
High (100 Hz–1 kHz)SEI layer impedanceR_SEI
Mid (1–100 Hz)Charge transfer resistance at electrodeRct
Low (<1 Hz)Diffusion (Warburg element)Z_w
Dataset
Cells16 NMC-811 lithium metal batteries — 8 training, 8 testing (small dataset!) EIS measurementsEIS spectrum recorded at multiple states-of-health across ageing cycles Features extractedReal impedance at various frequencies, imaginary impedance at various frequencies, phase angle ModelsXGBoost for capacity estimation; separate XGBoost for knee point prediction
SHAP Findings — Different Features for Different Tasks
Key insight: the most important EIS feature DIFFERS depending on what you're predicting.

For capacity estimation: Low-frequency imaginary impedance (Z_im at ~0.01–1 Hz) is most important. This represents the Warburg diffusion process — how well lithium ions diffuse through the electrolyte and electrodes. As capacity drops, diffusion slows because pores clog with SEI products and active material is lost.

For knee point prediction: High-frequency real impedance (Z_re at >1 kHz) is most important. This is the ohmic/SEI resistance. The knee point occurs when SEI suddenly grows faster or when lithium plating begins — both increase high-frequency resistance before the knee appears in the capacity curve.
The Knee Point Concept

Battery degradation follows a roughly S-shaped or L-shaped curve when you plot capacity vs. cycle number. Early in life, capacity drops slowly and linearly. Then at the "knee point," the degradation accelerates suddenly — you go from losing 0.05% capacity/cycle to losing 0.3% capacity/cycle. After the knee, the battery rapidly reaches end-of-life (80% SoH). Predicting when this knee occurs is extremely valuable for fleet management: you can schedule replacement 50–100 cycles before catastrophic decline rather than responding reactively.

Degradation Diagnostics for Lithium Ion Cells
Birkl, C.R., Roberts, M.R., McTurk, E., Bruce, P.G., Howey, D.A. · Journal of Power Sources, 2017 (submitted 2016) · DOI: 10.1016/j.jpowsour.2017.05.004
LCO+NCO Kokam 740mAh pouch OCV fitting / diagnostic algorithm Electrochemical model LLI / LAM Degradation modes 2016/2017
What This Paper Does — The Foundational Framework

This is the paper that formally established the LLI/LAM degradation diagnostic framework as a practical tool. Before this paper, researchers knew these degradation modes existed in theory, but Birkl et al. provided an algorithm to quantify exactly how much of the capacity loss comes from each mode using just the open-circuit voltage (OCV) curve — without dismantling the cell.

The Three Degradation Modes (Your Core Framework)
ModeFull NamePhysical CauseEffect on CellOCV Signature
LLI Loss of Lithium Inventory SEI growth traps Li+ ions; lithium plating creates dead lithium; Li deposits on separator Fewer Li+ ions available for intercalation → capacity fade OCV curve compresses/contracts; endpoints shift
LAM_PE Loss of Active Material — Positive Electrode Particle cracking in cathode (NMC, LCO); phase transition fatigue; chemical dissolution Fewer cathode sites → capacity fade; impedance rises Features from positive half-cell OCV diminish
LAM_NE Loss of Active Material — Negative Electrode Graphite exfoliation; lithium plating blockages; binder decomposition Fewer anode sites → capacity fade; especially severe at low temperature Features from negative half-cell OCV diminish
The Diagnostic Algorithm

The algorithm works by fitting a simulated OCV curve to the measured full-cell OCV curve. The simulated OCV is built from two half-cell OCV curves (measured separately): U_pe(x) for the positive electrode and U_ne(y) for the negative electrode. Three parameters (LLI, LAM_PE, LAM_NE) are adjusted until the simulated OCV matches the measured OCV. The values of these parameters at convergence tell you how much of each degradation mode has occurred.

Full-cell OCV ≈ U_pe(x) − U_ne(y), where x and y are stoichiometric coordinates that shift depending on LLI, LAM_PE, LAM_NE. By fitting this curve at different ageing stages, you track how much of each mode has accumulated.

Degradation Diagnostics for Commercial Lithium-Ion Batteries Used in Electric Vehicles
Birkl, C.R. et al. · Journal of the Electrochemical Society, 2017 · (Same Oxford group, companion paper)
LCO+NCO Kokam 740mAh pouch OCV diagnostics SEM / EDX validation Low temperature degradation LAM_NE dominance 2017
What This Paper Does

This is the companion experimental paper to Birkl JPS 2016. It applies the diagnostic algorithm to cells aged under specifically harsh conditions: −10°C temperature at 2C charge rate, conditions representative of EV operation in cold climates (UK winter, northern Europe). The key question: which degradation mode dominates under these extreme conditions?

Key Finding — LAM_NE Dominates at Low Temperature
At −10°C + 2C charging: LAM_NE (Loss of Active Negative Electrode Material) is the dominant degradation mode, not LLI. Why? At low temperature, lithium-ion diffusion in the electrolyte and through the graphite anode slows dramatically. The ions cannot intercalate fast enough, so they plate on the graphite surface as metallic lithium (lithium plating). This metallic lithium then reacts with electrolyte, creating dead lithium and blocking graphite pores — both of which count as LAM_NE.
Experimental Validation
  • SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy): Post-mortem imaging confirmed graphite surface deposits matching lithium plating morphology
  • EDX (Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy): Chemical analysis of surface deposits confirmed elemental lithium
  • 3-electrode cells: Separated anode and cathode measurements confirmed the anode degraded far more than the cathode

This is rare in literature: most degradation studies rely only on full-cell electrical measurements. Having physical post-mortem validation makes the diagnostic algorithm's conclusions credible.

Your Dissertation Connection
When your Oxford SoH data shows Cell5 degrading faster than Cell7, and when you note temperature effects in your fleet data, this paper gives you the mechanistic explanation: low-temperature operations (cold climate parking, winter charging) accelerate LAM_NE via lithium plating, not just LLI. This is why temperature appears in your Fleet Severity Index. Cite in: Chapter 2 (Literature Review — temperature-dependent degradation), Chapter 5 (Discussion — temperature effects in your FSI analysis).

Data-Driven Fast Clustering of Second-Life Lithium-Ion Batteries
Ran, A. et al. · Journal of Energy Storage, 2020
Mixed (retired EV batteries) Pulse test clustering Bisecting K-means Second-life batteries Battery sorting 2020
What This Paper Does

When EV batteries retire from vehicles (typically at 80% SoH), they can still be used for stationary energy storage (second life). But retired batteries from different vehicles, conditions, and histories have wildly different remaining capacities and internal resistances. This paper develops a fast data-driven method to sort these batteries into homogeneous groups for second-life applications.

The Problem With Standard Testing

Traditional full charge-discharge characterization of retired batteries takes 4–8 hours per cell and is expensive. When dealing with thousands of retired EV batteries, this is completely impractical. Ran et al.'s solution: use a short pulse test (applying known current pulses and measuring voltage response over minutes) plus clustering to group batteries in hours, not days.

Method
Input featuresVoltage response during short current pulses (5–10 minutes per battery) Clustering algorithmBisecting K-means (hierarchical variant that bisects the largest cluster iteratively) Accuracy88% match with full C/D characterization — reduces test time from hours to minutes Key degradation causes citedSEI growth, loss of active material, dead lithium accumulation, internal resistance increase
Why Clustering Works Here

The pulse test voltage response creates a "fingerprint" of each battery's internal state. Batteries that degraded similarly (same mechanism, similar severity) produce similar fingerprints and cluster together. Bisecting K-means finds these natural groupings without requiring you to know in advance how many groups exist.

Your Dissertation Connection
This paper demonstrates that clustering-based methods work for battery state assessment at scale — exactly the problem your dissertation addresses for fleet-level degradation analytics. The parallel is direct: they cluster retired batteries by degradation fingerprint; you cluster active fleet vehicles by duty-cycle severity fingerprint. Both enable differentiated, data-driven decision-making rather than treating all batteries identically. Cite in: Chapter 2 (Literature Review — data-driven clustering for battery management), Chapter 3 (Methodology — justification for using clustering in fleet analytics).

Python Battery Mathematical Modelling (PyBaMM)
Sulzer, V., Marquis, S.G., Timms, R., Robinson, M., Chapman, S.J. · Journal of Open Research Software, 2021 · DOI: 10.5334/jors.309
Python / Open-source DFN / SPMe / SPM Physics-based modelling SEI degradation model 2021
What This Paper Does

This is a software paper — it describes PyBaMM (Python Battery Mathematical Modelling), an open-source Python library developed by researchers at Oxford, Stanford, and Berkeley. It provides a modular, extensible framework for implementing physics-based battery models without reimplementing basic equations from scratch.

Models Available in PyBaMM
ModelFull NameComplexityUse Case
SPMSingle Particle ModelLowFast simulation, rough estimates
SPMeSingle Particle Model with ElectrolyteMediumIncludes electrolyte concentration effects — good for high C-rate and temperature effects
DFNDoyle-Fuller-NewmanHighFull porous electrode model — most accurate, but computationally expensive
The Expression Tree Architecture

PyBaMM's key engineering contribution is an "expression tree" system: model equations are represented as symbolic mathematical objects (like a computer algebra system) that can be differentiated, discretized, and solved numerically without the user writing manual derivative code. This makes adding degradation submodels straightforward — you define equations symbolically, and PyBaMM handles the numerical solution.

Degradation Submodels Available
  • SEI growth (solvent-diffusion model, reaction-limited model)
  • Lithium plating (kinetic model)
  • Particle cracking (mechanical fatigue model)
  • Electrode/electrolyte decomposition

A Constraint Programming Approach to Electric Vehicle Routing with Time Windows (EVRPTW)
Booth, K.E.C. & Beck, J.C. · CPAIOR 2019 · University of Toronto
Constraint Programming MILP EV fleet routing Charge scheduling 2019
What This Paper Does

This paper tackles the Electric Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (EVRPTW) — the problem of planning optimal routes for a fleet of EVs that must: (1) visit customers within specific time windows, (2) manage battery range anxiety, and (3) schedule recharging at available stations. The authors propose the first Constraint Programming (CP) formulations for this problem.

The EVRPTW Problem Structure
  • Nodes: Depot (start/end), Customer locations (demand), Charging stations (partial or full recharge)
  • Vehicle constraints: Battery capacity limit, energy consumption per unit distance, recharge time at stations
  • Time constraints: Arrive at customer within [earliest, latest] time window
  • Objective: Minimize total distance, number of vehicles, or time
CP vs. MILP — Why This Matters
ApproachStrengthWeakness
MILP (Mixed Integer Linear Programming)Finds high-quality solutions quickly for small-medium problems; optimal for distance minimization with long scheduling horizonsScales poorly to large fleets/many customers

Hybrid MILP-CPBest for distance minimization, large scheduling horizonsMore complex to implement
Why This Paper Exists in Your Reading List
This paper is about fleet-level operations, not battery degradation directly. The connection to your dissertation is operational: EVRPTW models show that route planning decisions (which roads, which sequence of customers, where to charge, when to charge) directly control how batteries are loaded. A fleet that optimises routes for minimal distance may inadvertently cause aggressive duty cycles; a fleet that optimises for battery health must consider degradation cost. Your dissertation's FSI (Fleet Severity Index) can be thought of as the degradation-awareness component missing from EVRPTW optimisations like this one.

The Multi-Depot Electric Vehicle Location Routing Problem with Time Windows (MDVLRP)
Paz, J.C., Granada-Echeverri, M., Escobar, J.W. · International Journal of Industrial Engineering Computations, 2018 · DOI: 10.5267/j.ijiec.2017.4.001
MILP Multi-depot EV routing Battery swapping Charging infrastructure 2018
What This Paper Does

This extends the standard EV routing problem to the multi-depot case with location decisions: not just "how to route vehicles" but also "where to place depots and charging stations." It's a planning-level optimisation model for EV logistics networks. The paper considers two energy supply technologies: conventional plug-in charging (partial or full) and Battery Swapping Stations (BSS).

Three MILP Models Proposed
  • Model 1 — Conventional charging only: Vehicles can charge at any node (depot, customer, special station); partial or full charge allowed
  • Model 2 — Battery Swapping Stations (BSS) only: BSS located only at special vertices (not customer nodes due to infrastructure complexity); instant battery swap
  • Model 3 — Mixed (both technologies): If a charging vertex is activated → BSS; if customer vertex → conventional charge
Key Insight — Routing and Location Decisions Are Coupled
The paper shows that routing decisions (which vehicles go where) and location decisions (where to put charging stations) are mutually dependent: placing a station further from typical routes increases detour time; detours change which routes are optimal. This interdependency means you can't solve them separately — you need a joint optimisation model. This is the paper's fundamental mathematical contribution.
Battery Swapping vs. Conventional Charging

BSS takes a fixed short time (battery swap is fast, but infrastructure is expensive and batteries must be standardised). Conventional charging time is a function of the amount of energy needed. The paper finds that BSS reduces fleet travel time substantially (no long charging waits) but requires careful placement because vehicles must detour specifically to BSS nodes, not customer nodes.

Theme 2: Clustering Reveals Operational Structure That Statistics Misses

Papers: İnan (DBSCAN for SoC), Ran (bisecting K-means for second-life sorting), your dissertation (DBSCAN for fleet duty cycle profiling)

A consistent finding across all clustering-based papers: the cluster label itself becomes a significant predictive feature. This means the operating regime matters beyond the raw measurements. Theme 3: LLI Is the Dominant Degradation Mode, But Temperature Changes the Story

Papers: Mulpuri (1), Birkl JPS (4), Birkl JES (5)

Under normal operating conditions (room temperature, moderate C-rate), LLI (Loss of Lithium Inventory — primarily from SEI growth) accounts for ~70% of capacity loss. But at low temperature (−10°C, 2C charge) or high charge rates, LAM_NE (graphite anode damage from lithium plating) can become equally or more important. This context-dependence is exactly why fleet severity classification matters: different fleets operating in different temperature regimes will experience fundamentally different degradation mechanisms, not just different rates of the same mechanism.

Theme 4: Physics-Based and Data-Driven Models Are Complementary

Papers: Mulpuri/Sulzer (physics — PyBaMM SPMe), İnan/Si (data-driven — LightGBM/XGBoost), Birkl (electrochemical diagnostics)

The literature has moved beyond treating physics-based and data-driven as competitors. Physics models (PyBaMM) provide mechanistic understanding and work with small datasets but require known parameters. Data-driven models (XGBoost, LightGBM) scale to large real-world datasets without knowing internal cell parameters but require explanability tools (SHAP) to trust their outputs. The state-of-the-art approach is hybrid: use physics models to generate synthetic training data or constrain ML model outputs, then use ML for real-time fleet-scale prediction.

Theme 5: Fleet Operations and Battery Degradation Are Currently Separate Research Fields

Papers: Booth (8), Paz (9) vs. all battery papers (1–7)

Booth and Paz optimise EV fleet routing (minimise distance, time, number of vehicles). Battery papers (1–7) quantify degradation mechanisms and predict SoH. Zero papers in this set bridge both domains. No paper says: "given a routing plan, what is the resulting battery degradation cost?" or "how should routes be modified to minimise total-cost-of-ownership including battery replacement?" This is not just a gap — it is the explicit research gap your dissertation fills.

Viva Defence Q&A — Paper-Specific Questions

Q: "Why do you use DBSCAN for clustering instead of K-means?"
A: "DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) was chosen for two reasons. First, it does not require specifying the number of clusters in advance — the number of fleet duty cycle groups emerges from the data. K-means requires you to specify k, which is arbitrary without prior knowledge of how many distinct fleet usage patterns exist. Second, DBSCAN handles noise and outlier driving events naturally by labelling them as noise points rather than forcing them into the nearest cluster. İnan et al. (2025) demonstrated this approach effectively for battery operating regime clustering, using adaptive ε selection via median absolute deviation — I applied the same methodology to fleet telemetry data."
Q: "How do you validate that your Fleet Severity Index actually predicts battery degradation?"
A: "I validate FSI in two ways. First, I cross-reference FSI scores computed from the experimental datasets (NASA, Oxford, CALCE) with measured SoH at known cycle counts — higher FSI conditions should correspond to steeper SoH decline, which I verify statistically. Second, the weighting coefficients (0.30 KI, 0.25 DoD, 0.25 Temperature, 0.20 C-peak) are grounded in the literature: Mulpuri et al. show KI is the strongest drive-cycle predictor of degradation; Birkl et al. show temperature is critical especially for LAM_NE; DoD and C-rate weights follow established degradation model literature. SHAP analysis of my trained ML model then provides data-driven confirmation of which FSI components contribute most — if SHAP rankings match literature expectations, the FSI design is validated."
Q: "You use NMC chemistry in your FSI design but your datasets (NASA, CALCE CS2/CX2) are LCO. Isn't that a contradiction?"
A: "Not a contradiction, but an acknowledged limitation. The degradation mechanisms are largely shared — LLI from SEI growth and LAM from particle cracking occur in both LCO and NMC chemistries. The FSI is designed around these universal mechanisms, so the index structure transfers across chemistries. However, the rate constants differ: NMC-811 (as studied by Mulpuri et al. and Si et al.) has higher energy density but is more vulnerable to mechanical stress and thermal runaway than LCO. My CALCE and NASA datasets provide extensive LCO cycling data for training and validation, and I note explicitly that the FSI coefficients should be recalibrated for NMC-specific fleet deployments using NMC-specific datasets, which I identify as a limitation and future work direction."
Q: "What is the knee point and why does it matter for fleet operators?"
A: "The knee point is the inflection in the capacity-vs-cycle-number curve where degradation transitions from slow linear decline to rapid nonlinear decline. Before the knee, a fleet operator might lose 0.05% capacity per cycle; after the knee, this accelerates to 0.3% per cycle or faster, rapidly approaching the 80% SoH end-of-life threshold. Si et al. (2025) showed that high-frequency real impedance from EIS predicts this knee point before it appears in the capacity curve. For fleet operators, the practical value is scheduling-driven: if you can predict 50–100 cycles before the knee that a specific vehicle's battery is approaching it, you can proactively reassign that vehicle to lighter duty cycles, reducing fleet risk. My FSI clusters enable this: vehicles in high-FSI clusters reach their knee points faster, giving a basis for predictive maintenance scheduling."
Q: "Booth (2019) and Paz (2018) are routing papers, not degradation papers. Why are they in your literature review?"
A: "Precisely because they illustrate the gap my dissertation fills. The routing literature treats battery capacity as a range constraint — vehicles must not run out of energy — but does not model how the specific routes chosen affect long-term battery health. Booth and Paz optimise for distance, time, and vehicle count. Neither incorporates a battery degradation cost term. My Fleet Severity Index provides exactly this missing component: a degradation-aware metric that quantifies the long-term battery wear cost of any given operational profile. This positions my work as a bridge between the fleet operations optimisation literature and the battery degradation literature — two communities that have not yet converged."
Q: "PyBaMM (Sulzer 2021) is a physics-based model. Your approach is data-driven. Are you saying physics-based models are wrong?"
A: "Not at all — they're complementary. Physics-based models like PyBaMM SPMe (as used by Mulpuri et al.) are mechanistically correct but require precise cell parameters (diffusivity, exchange current density, particle radius) that fleet operators don't have for their battery packs. My data-driven approach works with electrical measurements (voltage, current, temperature) that fleet telematics systems already collect. The two approaches validate each other: if my ML model assigns high degradation risk to high-KI, high-temperature driving patterns, and Mulpuri's PyBaMM simulation shows the same patterns cause 12–18% faster capacity loss, that convergence strengthens both findings. The ideal long-term approach — which I identify as future work — is a hybrid: use PyBaMM to generate synthetic training data for rare operating conditions, then use that to train the ML model on underrepresented cases."
